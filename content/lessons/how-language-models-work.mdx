## One job, done extremely well

When you type a question into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, it can feel like you're talking to someone. What's actually answering is a <Term slug="large-language-model">large language model</Term>, and its core skill is surprisingly simple.

<Definition term="Large Language Model">
  A neural network trained on an enormous amount of text to do one thing: **predict what comes next**. Ask it to continue "The capital of France is" and it predicts "Paris".
</Definition>

Everything else, answering questions, writing emails, summarising reports, translating, comes from doing that one job extremely well, one small piece at a time.

<Example title="You already do this">
  Finish these sentences in your head:

  - "Once upon a ..."
  - "Salt and ..."
  - "The meeting has been moved to next ..."

  You probably thought *time*, *pepper*, and *week* (or a day of the week). You didn't look anything up; years of reading and listening taught you what usually comes next. A language model learned the same kind of thing, from far more text than any person could read in a thousand lifetimes.
</Example>

## Step 1: Text becomes tokens

A model can't read letters the way you do. First, your text is chopped into small pieces called <Term>tokens</Term>.

<Definition term="Token">
  A small piece of text that a model reads and writes: often a whole common word, sometimes part of a longer word, a number, or a punctuation mark. In English, 100 tokens is roughly 75 words.
</Definition>

Common words like "the" or "cat" are usually one token. Rarer or longer words get split into familiar pieces, so "tokenization" might become "token" + "ization". This lets a model handle any word, even ones it has never seen, by building it from parts.

## Step 2: Tokens become numbers

Each token has an ID number in the model's vocabulary, a list of tens of thousands of possible pieces. Press **Show what the model sees** in the explainer above: that list of numbers is all the model ever receives.

Inside the model, each number is turned into a long list of other numbers that captures something about the token's meaning. Words used in similar ways, like "cat" and "dog", end up with similar lists. This is how a model "knows" that a cat and a dog have more in common than a cat and a carburettor.

## Step 3: Predict the next token

The numbers now flow through the model's layers: billions of <Term>parameters</Term>, adjusted during training. Out the other end comes a score for every token in the vocabulary: how likely it is to come next.

For "The cat sat on the", the scores might look like this:

- **mat**: 41%
- **floor**: 18%
- **sofa**: 12%
- **roof**: 6%
- ...and tens of thousands of others, each with a tiny chance

The model picks one, usually a likely one, but not always the single most likely. A little randomness is what makes answers sound natural, and why asking the same question twice can give different replies.

## Step 4: Repeat

Here's the part that surprises most people. The model only ever predicts **one token at a time**. To write a full answer, it:

1. Reads your message, as tokens
2. Predicts the next token
3. Adds that token to the end of the text
4. Reads the whole thing again, and predicts the next one
5. Repeats until it predicts "I'm done"

That's why chat assistants appear to type their answers word by word: they really are producing them in order. Using a trained model like this is called <Term>inference</Term>.

<KeyPoint>
  A language model writes by predicting the next token, adding it, and repeating, one small piece at a time. Everything it does is built on that loop.
</KeyPoint>

## How did it learn all this?

During training, the model was shown a huge amount of text (its <Term>training data</Term>): books, websites, articles and code, adding up to trillions of tokens. Over and over, it hid the next token from itself, made a guess, checked the real answer, and nudged its parameters to guess slightly better next time.

Do that trillions of times and something remarkable happens. To get good at predicting text, the model has to pick up grammar, facts, writing styles, and even some reasoning patterns, because all of those help predict what comes next.

## Why it sometimes makes things up

This also explains AI's best-known weakness. A model is trained to produce text that *sounds* likely, not text that is *true*. Usually those are the same. But when the model doesn't really "know" something, the most likely-sounding continuation can be a confident, fluent, completely invented answer.

This is often called **hallucination**. It's not a bug that will simply be fixed; it's a side effect of how these models work. That's why it's worth checking facts, figures and quotes that matter.

<TryIt title="Experiment with tokens">
  Use the explainer above to answer these questions for yourself.

  1. Type your first name. Is it one token or several? Common names are usually one; unusual ones get split.
  2. Type a very long word, like **internationalization**. How many pieces does it become?
  3. Type the year **2026**, then a long number like **1234567**. What happens to the digits?
  4. Type the same sentence twice, once with normal spaces and once with double spaces. Does the token count change?
  5. Compare a short, plain sentence with a long, formal one that says the same thing. Which uses more tokens?
</TryIt>

Token counts matter in practice: AI services often charge by the token, and every model has a limit on how many tokens it can consider at once (its "context window").
